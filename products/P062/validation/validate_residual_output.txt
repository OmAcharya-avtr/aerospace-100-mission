2. Uncertainty: coverage of the predictive interval
==============================================================================
The band is sqrt(GP predictive variance + thermometer variance), so it is
a predictive interval for a new measurement, not a confidence interval
for the mean function. Nominal 0.9500 and 0.6827.

  design                cov 95    cov 68  mean GP sd [K]
  baseline-eclipse      0.9709    0.7573        0.333346
  full-sun              0.9900    0.8313        0.305800
  lumped-nodes          0.9933    0.8437        0.299475
  weak-excitation       0.9900    0.8221        0.303518

Every number above is ABOVE nominal, so the interval is conservative --
too wide, not too narrow. That is the safer direction to be wrong in and
it is still wrong: an interval reported as 95 per cent that covers 97 to
99 per cent is not calibrated, and must not be quoted as a 95 per cent
interval. It is usable as a conservative bound and as a ranking signal.

  design              weak fraction   ratio to baseline

On the structurally non-identifiable design the learned residual puts 78.8 times
as large a share of its energy into directions the data cannot constrain
as it does on the sound design, AT THIS ONE CONFIGURATION. Both designs
show a similar held-out improvement: +0.34 against +0.39. That is what it
means to say an improvement is partly real and partly absorption, stated
as a number rather than as a caveat.

DO NOT QUOTE THAT RATIO ON ITS OWN. validate_absorption.py varies the
training-set size and the noise seed and finds the ratio swinging from
3.3 to 96.7, because the sound design's absorption fraction is small and
its run-to-run variation is a large share of itself. What survives every
configuration tried is a SEPARATION: the degenerate design's absorption
fraction stays in [0.08144, 0.15605] and the sound design's in
[0.00161, 0.02441], and the two ranges do not overlap, while the held-out
improvement does not separate the two designs at all.

HOW MUCH OF THIS DEPENDS ON THE CUT, stated plainly because it matters:
at the two usable cuts, 1e-2 and 1e-3, full-sun's absorption fraction is
one to three orders of magnitude above every other design's, and the
ordering is the same. At the loosest cut, 1e-1, the ordering INVERTS:
baseline-eclipse reads 0.356 and full-sun 0.161. That is not a
contradiction, it is the cut becoming meaningless -- at 1e-1 only ONE of
the six parameter directions is called strong on every design, so the
weak subspace is five-sixths of the parameter space and the measure stops
separating 'the data cannot constrain this' from 'the data constrains
this less well than the single best direction'. The singular spectra
below show why: the second singular value is already 6 to 9 per cent of
the first on every design, so a 1e-1 cut lands above almost everything.
Conclusion: the absorption measure is meaningful for cuts below the
spectrum's own second value and is reported at 1e-2 and 1e-3 for that
reason. The 1e-1 row is printed rather than dropped so that the
sensitivity of the conclusion to this choice is visible.

  parameter    physics only  with residual   log shift
  C1                846.746        844.959    -0.00211
  C2                4036.92        4142.22    +0.02575
  k12               2.65049        2.66579    +0.00576
  h_rad         1.30353e-08    1.35772e-08    +0.04073
  q_sun             55.1111        56.4072    +0.02325

  training chi2: 1628.25 -> 864
  RMS log error against truth: 0.132553 -> 0.178772

This is the finding that must not be softened. On baseline-eclipse the
residual model reduces the held-out error by 39 per cent and makes the
physical parameters WORSE by 35 per cent in root-mean-square log error.
If the deliverable is a prediction, use it. If the deliverable is a
physical parameter -- a heat capacity to put in a thermal report -- the
residual model is actively harmful and the physics-only fit, with its
profile intervals, is the thing to report.
